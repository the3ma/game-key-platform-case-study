From paid order to delivered game key — automatically, and never bought twice.

A retailer sells digital game keys on its Shopify store and buys each one from a wholesale distributor. I designed and built the platform between them: it keeps the catalog and every price in sync, buys the key the moment an order is paid, and delivers it through a secure customer portal — with an audit trail behind every step.

Executive summary

The whole story in thirty seconds.

The problem

Every paid order needed a key bought from a supplier and delivered by hand, across thousands of products priced per country and currency. Mistakes cost real money: a key bought twice is paid twice.

The approach

Map every flow by what its failure would cost, then design the failure paths first: claim before buying, queue every external call, give every unknown outcome a named state and a human fix.

The outcome

Orders become delivered keys with no one in the loop; staff see and fix the rare exception themselves; any order or price change can be traced end to end.

Context & problem

The retailer’s product is not stock on a shelf. It is a code bought from a supplier on demand, so the business is only as good as the link between the store and the supplier.

  1. Shopify storeCustomers browse and pay
  3. The platformSyncs the catalog, buys and delivers keys
  5. Key distributorSells keys, prices and stock per region

Without the platform

  • Thousands of products whose prices differ per country and currency and change daily at the supplier.
  • Stock is regional: a key sold into the wrong region is a refund and an unhappy customer.
  • Every paid order needs someone to buy the key and send it by hand.
  • A key bought twice is paid twice; a wrong price is sold at a loss.
  • No answer to “what happened to this order?” or “who changed this price?”

What the business needed

  • The store always shows what the supplier can deliver, at the right price for each market.
  • A paid order becomes a delivered key with no human in the loop.
  • When something does go wrong, staff see it and fix it without a developer.
  • Every money-relevant action is traceable end to end.
  • A key is only ever shown to the customer who bought it.

Discovery: mapping the risk

Before choosing tools, I traced every flow end to end — orders, bulk purchases, catalog and price syncs, admin actions, customer sessions — and ranked each one by what a failure or a misuse would cost.

TierWhat it protectsExamplesRule
CriticalMoney, a customer’s key, the price we sell at, admin accessOrder → key, manual key handling, bulk orders, catalog and pricing, sign-inTraceable end to end
ImportantKey codes, business data, configuration, dispute evidenceCSV exports, destructive store changes, old and new values, who viewed a keyShould be traceable
Nice to haveOperations and investigationsScheduled sync summaries, health signalsTraced where cheap

23 flows were ranked this way. The ranking decided what had to be designed first, what the audit log records, and where a person stays in the loop. No user research was run; this is the project’s own risk analysis.

What the mapping showed

Four findings shaped everything that followed.

  • The happy path is easy; the money is in the failure modes.Retries, redelivered webhooks and stalled jobs are normal in queue-based systems. Each one is a chance to pay twice.
  • “No answer” is not “not bought”.When the supplier times out, the purchase may have gone through. Software cannot know; it has to stop and ask a person.
  • Non-developers run the business.Operations and finance staff must find and fix problems from the admin portal, or every exception becomes a developer ticket.
  • The key is the product.A key in an email, a log line or an audit entry is a key that can leak. It may only reach the customer, through an authenticated page.

Who it serves

Three groups, three different jobs. Their pain points became the platform’s requirements.

Operations staff

Run the daily business in the admin portal

Job
Resolve pending and failed keys, manage products, prices, promotions and bulk orders, watch system health.
Pain points
Actions trigger background syncs whose results arrive later; writes reach the live store; dense multi-currency data.
What they got
Problems visible on the dashboard with a one-click fix, and clear confirmation before any action that touches the store or the supplier.

Finance

Report on revenue and margins

Job
Pull reports, VAT, exchange rates, partner revenue share and key-storage figures.
Pain points
Numbers must be exact across currencies, and getting them used to mean asking a developer.
What they got
Exact, sortable figures and CSV exports, each export recorded in the audit log.

Customers

Buy a game on the store

Job
Get the key they paid for, quickly and safely.
Pain points
Waiting with no news; keys sent in plain email that anyone can forward.
What they got
An email with a link, sign-in with their existing store account, and the key shown only after an ownership check.

Design decisions

Each decision starts from a business risk, not a technology.

A key is never bought twice

Money-critical
Risk
Jobs are retried, stall or are re-queued; a naive worker calls the supplier again and pays for a second key.
Decision
Claim the key (PENDING → PROCESSING) before calling the supplier; keep the supplier’s answer in the job until the database has it; make the order webhook idempotent.
Result
At-least-once infrastructure, exactly-once spending.

Every failure has a named state and a fix

Money-critical
Risk
A refused, timed-out or unknown purchase silently disappears.
Decision
An explicit key lifecycle. A timeout leaves the key PROCESSING with a reason on the dashboard; staff enter it by hand. Refused keys can be retried; keys whose job never started are re-queued by a sweep.
Result
No lost order, and no customer waiting on a developer.

Bulk purchases run on a schedule, just as safely

Risk
Hundreds of keys bought unattended to fill key storage, spread over hours.
Decision
Split each bulk order into scheduled operations with one delayed job each; claim before buying; send the operation id as the request id on every attempt; save operation and key in one transaction.
Result
Large purchases run on their own, and none can be paid twice.

Everything slow or external goes through a queue

Risk
The supplier, the store and the email provider are slower and less reliable than our own code.
Decision
Redis-backed BullMQ queues with dedicated workers. Workers throw only on temporary errors, so the queue retries them, and finish on permanent ones.
Result
A webhook is answered in milliseconds; a partner outage delays work instead of losing it.

Email is a queued side effect, never a blocker

Risk
The email provider is down, but a bought key must stay bought, and a key email is sensitive.
Decision
Callers only enqueue. A worker sends with backoff retries; a bad address stops at once; if the queue is unavailable the email goes out directly. The audit keeps the receipt, never the content.
Result
No purchase ever waits for, or fails because of, email.

Change-only, scheduled catalog and price sync

Risk
Thousands of products and per-market prices against a rate-limited store API.
Decision
Scheduled syncs from the supplier, one price job per product, batched by price list and skipped when unchanged; no product goes live without a base-currency price.
Result
The store sells what can be delivered, at the intended price.

An audit trail that can never break an order

Risk
Disputes need evidence, but logging must not slow down or fail the order it describes.
Decision
Append-only audit fed by its own queue: every admin write, order step and customer action, with a trace id, old and new values, revert with a conflict check, and secrets masked.
Result
One search tells an order’s full story, in order.

Safe by default, for staff and customers

Risk
One click can wipe live store configuration; a guessed URL could expose someone else’s key.
Decision
Destructive store actions need a confirmation token and alert all admins. The portal uses store sign-in (OAuth + PKCE), checks ownership on every request and rate-limits key actions.
Result
The platform protects the business from its own mistakes.

Lessons learned

What I would carry into the next system that moves money.

  • Design the failure path first.The happy path is the small part; the failure paths are where the design actually lives.
  • Make “unknown” a first-class state.A state that says “we don’t know, a person must check” is safer than any clever retry.
  • Side effects must never block the money path.Logging and email are important, but neither may slow down or undo a purchase.
  • Rank by business risk before deciding what to trace.Tracing everything is noise; tracing what can cost money is evidence.
