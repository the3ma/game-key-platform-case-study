From paid order to delivered game key — automatically, safely, and never bought twice.

An online retailer sells digital game keys on its Shopify store and buys each key from a wholesale key distributor. I designed and built the platform that connects the two: it keeps the whole catalog and every price in sync, buys a key the moment an order is paid, and delivers it to the customer through a secure portal — with a full audit trail behind every step.

  • My roleArchitect & full-stack engineer
  • ScopeAPI + workers, admin portal, customer portal, infrastructure
  • StackNestJS, Vue 3, PostgreSQL, Redis / BullMQ, Docker
  • ClientAnonymized (private codebase)

The business problem

The retailer’s product is not physical stock — it is a code bought from a supplier on demand. That makes the business only as good as the link between the store and the supplier.

Without the platform

  • Thousands of products whose prices differ per country and currency, and change daily at the supplier.
  • Stock is per region: a key sold in the wrong region is a refund and an unhappy customer.
  • Every paid order needs someone to buy the key at the supplier and send it by hand.
  • Mistakes cost real money: a key bought twice is paid twice; a wrong price is sold at a loss.
  • No answer to “what happened to this order?” or “who changed this price?”

What the business needed

  • The store always shows what the supplier can actually deliver, at the right price for each market.
  • A paid order turns into a delivered key with no human in the loop.
  • When something does go wrong, staff can see it and fix it themselves — without a developer.
  • Every money-relevant action is traceable end to end.
  • Customer keys are only ever shown to the customer who bought them.

What made it hard

The happy path is simple. The real work was in the failure modes — each of them costs money or trust.

  • The supplier can go silent mid-purchaseA timeout does not mean “not bought”. Retrying blindly could pay for the same key twice.
  • Webhooks arrive more than onceThe store redelivers “order paid” events. Each one must create exactly one order.
  • There is no safe sandboxThe store is live: any write changes what real customers see and pay.
  • Prices are a moving targetMany currencies, per-country catalogs, promotions and VAT, synced from an external source on a schedule.
  • Non-developers run the businessOperations and finance staff must find and fix problems from the admin portal.
  • The key is the productA leaked key is a lost sale. It must never sit in an email, a log or an audit entry.

Key decisions

Each decision starts from a business risk, not a technology. Problem → what I chose → why it matters.

1. A key is never bought twice

Problem
Jobs get retried, stall or are re-queued. A naive worker would call the supplier again and pay for a second key.
Decision
The worker claims the key (PENDING → PROCESSING) before calling the supplier, so any repeat skips it. The supplier’s answer is kept in the job until the database has it, so a failed write is retried without buying again. Incoming orders are idempotent: a redelivered webhook inserts nothing.
Why it matters
Turns “at-least-once” infrastructure into “exactly-once” spending. The most expensive class of bug is designed out, not monitored for.

2. Every failure has a named state and a fix

Problem
When the supplier refuses, times out or does not know a product, the order must not silently disappear.
Decision
An explicit key lifecycle (see the state diagram below). A timeout leaves the key in PROCESSING with a reason, shown on the dashboard; staff check the supplier and enter the key by hand. Refused keys can be retried. Keys whose job never started are re-queued automatically by a scheduled sweep.
Why it matters
No order is lost and no customer waits on a developer. Support work becomes a short, visible queue instead of an investigation.

3. Bulk purchases run on a schedule, with the same money safety

Problem
Staff need to buy many keys at once to fill the retailer’s own key storage — spread across a time window instead of in one burst, and with nobody watching each purchase.
Decision
A bulk order is split into operations spread across the chosen window, each with its own delayed job. At its time the worker claims the operation (QUEUED → RUNNING) before buying, sends the operation id as the request id on every attempt, and writes the operation and its key in one transaction. Booked keys are revealed in a separate step and stored. A running operation can’t be cancelled or rescheduled; an unknown outcome is settled by staff (Enter key / Not sold).
Why it matters
Hundreds of purchases run unattended over hours, and none of them can be paid twice. Exporting the stored key codes is itself an audited action.

4. Everything slow or external goes through a queue

Problem
The supplier, the store and the email provider are all slower and less reliable than our own code.
Decision
Redis-backed BullMQ queues with dedicated workers for booking, bulk purchases, catalog sync, per-product price sync, email and audit writes. Workers throw only on temporary errors, so the queue retries them, and finish cleanly on permanent ones, so a bad record never retries forever.
Why it matters
A webhook is answered in milliseconds; an outage at a partner delays work instead of losing it.

5. Email is a queued side effect, never a blocker

Problem
The email provider can be slow or down, but a key that has been bought must stay bought — and a key email is sensitive.
Decision
A queued sender: callers only call MailService.send(), which adds a job and returns. An email worker renders the template and sends it through the provider’s API. Temporary failures (5xx, rate limit, network) retry from 30 s, doubling, 8 attempts over about two hours; a bad or inactive address stops at once. If the queue itself is unavailable, the email is sent directly. Every outcome is audited with the template, the recipient and the provider’s message id — never the content.
Why it matters
No request or purchase ever waits for, or fails because of, email — and there is proof of delivery for every message without a key ever reaching the logs.

6. Change-only, scheduled catalog and price sync

Problem
Thousands of products, prices per currency and country, per-market catalogs — and the store’s API is rate-limited.
Decision
Scheduled syncs pull products, prices and stock from the supplier, enrich them with game metadata, and push to the store. Prices go one job per product, batched by price list and skipped when unchanged; a product without a base-currency price is never published.
Why it matters
The store always sells what can actually be delivered, at the intended price, without hitting API limits.

7. An audit trail that answers “what happened?” and can never break an order

Problem
Disputes and pricing mistakes need evidence; but logging must not slow down or fail the order it describes.
Decision
An append-only audit log fed through its own queue. Every admin write, every order step and every customer action is recorded with a trace id; old and new values are kept for configuration changes, which can be reverted with a conflict check. Secrets and key codes are masked before storage. Flows are ranked by business risk (critical / important / nice to have) to decide what must be traced.
Why it matters
One search shows an order’s full story — webhook, booking attempts, staff actions, customer steps — in order.

8. Safe by default for people and for customers

Problem
A single admin click can wipe live store configuration; a guessed URL could expose someone else’s key.
Decision
Destructive store operations require an explicit confirmation token and notify all admins. The customer portal signs in with the store’s own customer accounts (OAuth + PKCE), checks order ownership on every request and rate-limits key actions. Emails carry a portal link, never the key.
Why it matters
The platform protects the business from its own mistakes and the customer’s purchase from everyone else.

Outcome

What changed for the business.

  • Hands-off fulfilmentA paid order becomes a delivered key without anyone touching it.
  • No double spending by designRetries, redelivered webhooks and supplier timeouts cannot buy the same key twice.
  • Problems are visible and fixablePending, failed and stuck keys show on the dashboard with a one-click fix, so operations staff handle support themselves.
  • A catalog that stays trueProducts, stock and prices per market follow the supplier on a schedule, without manual edits.
  • Traceability end to endOne search reconstructs any order or configuration change; finance gets its numbers without asking a developer.
  • Protected keysKeys reach only their owner, through an authenticated, ownership-checked portal — never by email or in logs.

What I would do next

  • Record the remaining planned audit points (order completion, bulk-order bookings, rejected requests at the auth guard).
  • Summaries for every scheduled sync run, so a silent sync failure is as visible as a failed order.
  • Automate production database migrations behind the same review gate as the code.
